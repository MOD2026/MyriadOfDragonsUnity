## 1. Context correction that reframed the discussion

Earlier analysis in this project assumed a PvE-first, solo game with fixed enemy formations.
That assumption was wrong.

**Actual direction:** a 24/7 live global multiplayer game. Formations are fixed only for
campaign and event grinding. Against live players and in guild wars, formations are dynamic
player choices.

This changes the information model materially and is the reason several earlier conclusions
were revised.

## 2. Battle screen — proposed structure

### 2.1 Core insight

Given MOS §6 (Formation → Combat → Resolved, formation locks, reinforcement windows at
clashes 4 and 8), the battle screen is approximately **95% spectator, 5% interaction**.

Consequence: it is not a control panel. Screen budget should go to legibility of state, not
to controls. Most interaction belongs on a separate pre-battle Formation screen.

**Recommendation: two screens, not one.**

- **Formation screen** (pre-battle, full interaction): full card art, placement, previews,
  lane composition, matchup information. This is where illustration quality is showcased.
- **Battle screen** (spectator + two interrupts): dense, legible, cinematic.

Attempting to serve both from one screen is the main reason portrait feels impossible.

### 2.2 Geometry problem and resolution

Six bands of three slots = 18 units on screen. On a ~390x844pt portrait device, after safe
areas, HUD, two avatar strips and a bottom bar, roughly 68pt height per row and ~116pt width
per slot remain.

That is a landscape-shaped tile. Card art is portrait-shaped. They do not fit.

**Resolution:** cards and board units are different objects with different rules.

- Card in hand / formation screen = full portrait illustration
- Unit on battle board = bust crop (~52pt, circular or rounded-square) + adjacent stat block

Secondary benefits: bust crops avoid the burned-in "Myriad of Dragons" watermark present in
the corner of existing card art, and they scale better than shrinking full portraits.

**Rarity 5–7 units** occupy two slots per MOS, producing a ~240x68 tile. The wider silhouette
becomes the visual signal of a heavy unit — mechanic reading as art.

### 2.3 Element placement rationale (condensed)

| Element | Position | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Clash track (1–12) | Top centre | Serves as match timer, progress bar AND reinforcement indicator in one element. Read constantly, never touched. |
| Avatar strips | Enemy top, player bottom | Groups all "side state" (avatar, HP, energy, fallen count). Maps to physical intuition. |
| HP | Bar + numeral | Bar for glanceable ratio, numeral for endgame precision. Neither alone is sufficient. |
| Energy | Inline in avatar strip | Energy is a side resource, not a lane resource, despite being generated by back-lane units. |
| Board (6 bands) | Centre | Proximity to contact line encodes lane role. Front lanes meet in the middle. Portrait's genuine advantage over landscape. |
| Contact band | Centre, ~40pt | Weather/global status affects both sides; centre is semantically neutral. Also hosts one-line event ticker. |
| Speed / auto / log | Bottom right | Easiest thumb zone. |
| Settings | Top left | Deliberately hardest reach — rare use, costly if mis-tapped mid-battle. |
| Battle log | Sheet, not persistent | Minority feature; should not consume permanent space. |
| Graveyard | Badge on avatar strip, tap to expand | No permanent space until a mechanic depends on it. |
| Card preview | Long-press any unit | Where full illustration gets its moment during battle. |

### 2.5 Screen hierarchy (visual priority order)

1. Clash track — where am I in the match
2. Both HP bars — am I winning
3. Contact band — what is happening right now
4. My board
5. Enemy board
6. Controls
7. Everything else — on demand

### 2.6 UX flow

```
Campaign node / matchmaking
  → Formation screen (placement, matchup info)
  → LOCK FORMATION
  → Battle: clashes 1-3 auto
  → Clash 4: reinforcement window, timed (or skip)
  → clashes 5-7 auto
  → Clash 8: reinforcement window
  → clashes 9-12, or avatar reaches 0 HP
  → Result → rewards → continue / retry / exit
```

### 2.7 Known problems with this proposal

1. Card illustrations barely appear during battle. Partly mitigated by the formation screen
   and a proposed finisher moment (board recedes, killing unit's full art fills screen ~1.2s).
2. 18 units is dense; a small-screen (5.4") variant is required.
3. Two-slot units break grid rhythm — intentional but complicates layout and animation.
4. Low spectator/streaming value in portrait. Matters if marketing needs gameplay footage.
5. The timed reinforcement window is the only pressure point; needs early playtest for stress
   and missed-window frustration.

### 2.8 Alternative layouts considered

- **A. Landscape battle only** — solves density and spectator appeal; breaks one-handed play.
- **B. Lane-focus cinematic** — camera pushes into the clashing lane pair; most premium, best
  spectator value; loses overview, slower matches, more animation cost.
- **C. Asymmetric compression** — player board full size, enemy board condensed to lane strips;
  nearly doubles available space; reduces enemy information (bad if reinforcement decisions
  depend on reading the enemy board).

**Preferred:** the portrait layout above, adopting B's cinematic push-in ONLY for reinforcement
windows and the finishing blow.

### 2.9 Accessibility requirements (non-negotiable)

- Faction identity (Andras / Ktini / Pnevmas) must carry **shape and icon**, never colour alone.
  Element advantage decides matches, so colour-only differentiation is a fairness defect, not a
  polish issue.
- Reduced-motion setting must **disable** impact shake, not shorten it (vestibular trigger).
- Board tiles show numerals, not sentences, so text scaling does not break layout.
- Touch targets: 48dp Android / 44pt iOS minimum.

## 3. Information model — what the enemy player can see before locking

### 3.1 The unanswered question that blocks this decision

**Is PvP asynchronous or synchronous?** These are different games and must not be conflated.

- **Asynchronous** (mobile norm): players save a defence formation, attacked while offline.
  Formations are static for days or weeks. Top defence formations get screenshotted, shared
  and countered — concealment only delays the wiki.
- **Synchronous live**: both players present, locking in the moment. Hidden information creates
  a genuine read. But requires real-time matchmaking at global scale, server-authoritative
  deterministic combat (MOS §14), latency handling, reconnection, and sufficient concurrency
  in every rank bracket at every hour.

"24/7 global multiplayer" is compatible with both. Nothing below can be finalised until this
is answered.

Preliminary view: for portrait, one-handed, 28–45 audience on commutes, **async is the more
likely correct call** — in which case the design question becomes "how do we stop defence
formations being solved," not "how much do we hide."

### 3.2 Hidden information only creates depth when players have priors

Depth from concealment requires the player to reason about what is hidden — probability, tells,
meta-knowledge, opponent modelling. Concealment without inference tools produces guessing, and
punished guessing reads as unfairness rather than tension.

**Therefore the real requirement is an inference layer**, not more hiding:

- opponent rank
- recent formation history / tendencies
- faction preference
- guild affiliation

Hide the specifics, expose the tendencies. Then concealment becomes reading.

### 3.3 Recommended reveal rules by mode

| Mode | Reveal | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| PvE / campaign | Lane totals + faction composition; hide card identities | Fixed enemies get solved regardless; full concealment only adds a mandatory first loss. |
| Async PvP | Defender's lane totals, faction spread, rank; hide identities | Static formations will be shared publicly anyway; aggregate info enables a real read while specifics retain surprise. |
| Live sync PvP | One row revealed — **attacker chooses which row to scout** | A choice is a decision; a random reveal is noise. Requires the inference layer in 3.2. |
| Guild wars | Reveal least; scouting is a **guild activity** | Members spend attacks to reveal rows, intel shared guild-wide. Rewards coordination. Strongest differentiator identified in this session. |

**Difficulty scaling must raise enemy quality, not lower player information.** Information
denial lowers player control rather than raising the skill ceiling, and produces "the game
feels random at high level" feedback.

### 3.4 Arguments evaluated

**Valid:**

- Concealment prevents pre-battle from becoming a solvable puzzle.
- In PvP, the hidden content is another player's evolving choice, not a table row — so the
  replayability argument holds in multiplayer even though it fails in PvE.
- Guild-wide scouting is a genuinely strong, socially-driven differentiator.

**Rejected or requiring revision:**

- "Same stage plays differently because content is hidden" — false for fixed PvE enemies.
  Variance there comes from randomising enemy formations, not from hiding them.
- "Higher difficulty reveals less" — fake difficulty; reduces attributability of outcomes to
  skill and frustrates the best-retained players.
- Both sides hiding two of three rows with simultaneous lock and no read available = coin flip
  presented as strategy. Worst possible property for competitive play.

### 3.5 Monetisation constraint (MOS P2 risk)

Scouting/reveal abilities sold for gems or gated behind gacha constitute **paid access to
competitive information** — a direct violation of MOS P2 (money buys speed, not exclusive
competitive power).

**Required rule:** in any competitive mode, scouting capability must be earned in-mode — by
rank, by guild contribution, or by consumables obtained through play. Never purchasable with
real money or premium currency.

This should be added to MOS §9 before any shop content is designed.

## 4. Battle length — flagged as incorrect

A figure of **10–15 minutes per battle** appeared in discussion. This appears unfounded and
should be corrected before it shapes design.

Twelve clashes at a few seconds each is **60–180 seconds**.

Fifteen-minute battles break:

- one-handed commute play (stated audience context)
- "fast navigation" (UI/UX bible)
- guild wars, which need many battles inside a war window
- live synchronous matches (mobile disconnection/abandonment rates over 15 minutes)
- ranked climbing pace
- any stamina economy

**Recommended target: 90–180 seconds per battle.** If a 10–15 minute engagement is desired,
the unit should be a *guild war session* composed of many short battles, not a single battle.

## 5. Esports as a design constraint — recommend removing

A 3x3 auto-resolving battle with two interrupt points has a structurally low skill-expression
ceiling. That is the deliberate consequence of one-handed portrait auto-battle, which is the
correct choice for the stated audience.

Esports demands high skill ceilings, high APM, deep information and spectator legibility.
Every one of those pushes against portrait, one-handed, auto-resolving and hidden information.

**Recommendation:** pursue competitive guild-war leaderboards and ranked ladders (achievable
and aligned). Remove esports from the constraint list, or accept it implies a different
product for a different audience.

## 6. Governance note

During this session there were two instructions to design "unconstrained by MOS" and to
"not review previous documents."

This is the exact failure mode MOS was created to prevent (§15 golden rule; §22 amendment
rule). A constitution suspended whenever a design conversation becomes interesting provides
no protection.

**Correct pattern:** design ambitiously, then route proposals through §22 as formal amendments
stating old rule, new rule, reason, affected systems, migration impact, test impact, and
player-data compatibility. This costs one paragraph and preserves the value of governance.

**Note on ordering:** the reveal rule (#5) cannot be correctly decided before the reinforcement
mechanic (#3). The value of pre-battle information depends on what the player can do about it
mid-battle. If reinforcement allows counter-placement, concealment is survivable; if it only
permits healing, concealment is pure punishment with no recovery lever.

## 8. Next design session — proposed scope

**Reinforcement mechanic.** Three candidate systems (not five; additional candidates would be
padding rather than analysis), each covering:

- core loop and why it is fun
- strategic depth and skill expression
- behaviour in async PvP and guild wars as first-class criteria
- complexity, AI difficulty, balance risk
- cost to prototype

Evaluated against real constraints: portrait, one-handed, simultaneous resolution, 12-clash
cap, 90–180 second battles, MOS §6 battle contract, live multiplayer as endstate.

Deliverable: one recommendation, plus any required MOS amendments written in §22 format.
Settlement method: prototype the preferred candidate in the browser mock and validate by
simulation, per MOS §17 (structural tests can stay green while balance regresses).
